The First Serve Advantage

How one shot shapes victory in professional tennis — and why landing it is only half the battle.

By Andre Ajed and Traian Cherciu

In professional tennis, matches can be decided by just a few points which is a very small margin. A missed first serve, a double fault, or an ace at the right moment can entirely change the momentum of a match. At the highest level of men's professional tennis, where players normally hit serves that go over 120 miles per hour, the first serve is one of the most important weapons a player has.

However, does making more first serves actually increase a player's chances of winning? Or is winning the points after those serves more important? Statistics from the Association of Tennis Professionals (ATP) suggest that there is a meaningful difference between simply getting a first serve in and making that serve effective. Players such as Alexander Zverev, Jannik Sinner, and Hubert Hurkacz demonstrate how different serving styles or techniques can create successful results. As professional tennis becomes increasingly driven by statistics and technology, the numbers behind the first serve offer a better understanding of what separates winning performances from losing ones.

Getting the Serve In vs. Winning the Point

To really understand how important the first serve is, two statistics must be distinguished which are first-serve percentage and first-serve points won. First-serve percentage measures how often a player's first serve lands inside the appropriate service box. First-serve points won measures how often a player wins the point after successfully landing that first serve. Although the two statistics sound relatively the same, they actually tell different sides of the story.

A player might land 65% of their first serves but win only 55% of those points. Another player might land just 50% of first serves but win 70% of those points. The first player is more consistent at getting the serve in, while the second is more successful at turning those serves into points. This distinction is specifically essential in professional tennis, where a strong and well-placed first serve can put the opponent under instant pressure. Winning points rapidly also reduces the number of opportunities an opponent has to take control of a rally.

According to the ATP's official serving statistics, the best players do not necessarily lead every serving category. If we take a look at Novak Djokovic for example, there are so many players that serve better than him but can't produce the same overall level that he can. Some rely on accuracy and consistency while others depend more heavily on the effectiveness of their first serve.

What the ATP Numbers Reveal

The 2024 ATP season provides an interesting comparison between three of the tour's most powerful servers. Alexander Zverev landed 71.3% of his first serves and won 76.7% of the points played behind them. Jannik Sinner, meanwhile, landed only 61.4% of his first serves but won 79.4% of those points. Hubert Hurkacz fell between the two in first-serve accuracy, landing 63.2% of his first serves and winning 77.7% of the points behind them.

These statistics reveal something notable about serving effectiveness. Zverev landed nearly 10 percentage points more first serves than Sinner, but Sinner was more successful at winning points once his first serve landed. However, the comparison becomes more interesting when looking at service games won.

According to the same ATP leaderboard, Sinner won 91.4% of his service games in 2024, compared with Zverev's 90.0% and Hurkacz's 88.7%. This suggests that landing a high percentage of first serves is not the only factor behind successful serving performances. Sinner's ability to win points behind both his first and second serves helped him maintain an extremely high service-game winning percentage despite landing fewer first serves than Zverev. For players competing at the highest level, the difference between landing a serve and winning the point behind it can be crucial.

Why First-Serve Points Won Matter

Scientific research provides additional evidence that winning points behind the first serve plays a critical role in professional tennis. A 2023 study published in PLOS ONE examined 4,669 points from the later rounds of three Grand Slam tournaments during the 2021 season. They found that players won approximately 75% of points behind their first serves on grass and hard courts compared with 69% on clay courts.

In contrast, players won approximately 55% of points behind their second serves across all three surfaces. This difference shows the advantage of having a successful first serve. When players are restricted to rely on their second serve, their chances of winning the point usually go down. The same study found that approximately 80% of points including a first serve and a short rally were won by the player that was serving.

To put this in a different perspective, the first serve can create an advantage before the rally has even developed. Another study, published in Trends in Sport Sciences in 2020 examined men's singles matches at the Australian Open. Researchers found that first-serve points won was among the statistics that distinguished winners from losers in both early and later tournament rounds. The findings support the theory that winning points behind the first serve is closely connected to successful match performances.

However, neither study establishes a universal percentage chance of winning an entire match based on first-serve statistics by itself. They illustrate the significance of first-serve effectiveness without proving that any single serving statistic guarantees victory.

Technology Is Changing How Tennis Is Analyzed

Professional tennis has become increasingly dependent on technology to measure player performance. The ATP uses statistical tracking systems and analytical tools to examine serving accuracy, point outcomes, rally patterns, and other aspects of the game. These measurements allow coaches and players to identify strengths and weaknesses that might not be obvious simply by watching a match.

In an August 2024 ATP analysis, tennis analyst Craig O'Shannessy examined a measurement that combines first-serve percentage with first-serve points won. Craig is a tennis expert that I've actually received a clinic from when I was in high school and it is safe to say he was very knowledgeable about the numbers. The calculation multiplies the percentage of first serves landed by the percentage of those points won. For example, a player landing 70% of first serves and winning 75% of those points would produce a combined first-serve rating of 52.5. This means the player wins 52.5% of all service points specifically through successful first serves, with the remaining points involving second serves.

The measurement helps explain why focusing on only one serving statistic can be misleading. A player who consistently lands first serves but struggles to win points may not be as effective as someone who lands slightly fewer first serves but wins a much greater percentage of those points. By combining the two statistics, analysts can better evaluate how frequently a player's first serve produces a successful outcome.

The First Serve Doesn't Tell the Whole Story

Although serving statistics provide valuable information, tennis matches involve much more than the serve. Returning ability, second-serve performance, court surface, physical endurance, and performance during important points all influence the final result. For example, a player might win a high percentage of first-serve points but struggle to break the opponent's serve. Another player might have less impressive serving statistics but compensate with stronger returning and baseline play.

The importance of the second serve also cannot be ignored. In a separate ATP analysis of Jannik Sinner and Carlos Alcaraz, the tour reported that players won an average of approximately 51% of points behind their second serves. Even a small improvement in that percentage can become meaningful over the course of a long match. This is why first-serve statistics are better understood as indicators of performance rather than exact predictions of match outcomes.

A player's first-serve percentage might increase during a match without necessarily improving their chances of winning if the opponent continues to return those serves effectively. Similarly, a player with a lower first-serve percentage might still win comfortably by dominating return games or performing better during break points. The relationship between serving statistics and match victories is therefore more complicated than a single percentage.

What Really Matters in Winning Matches

The available evidence points toward an important conclusion: getting the first serve in matters, but winning the points behind that serve is essential to turning it into a competitive advantage. ATP statistics show that successful players can have very different first-serve percentages while maintaining similarly strong serving performances. Research also demonstrates that points played behind first serves are won substantially more frequently than points played behind second serves.

Still, the published statistics examined here do not establish that a player with a particular first-serve percentage has a specific probability of winning the match. Answering that question would require analyzing individual match results alongside each player's serving statistics. What the evidence does show is that the first serve creates opportunities, but its effectiveness depends on what happens next.

For professional tennis players, simply landing the ball inside the service box is not enough. The real advantage comes from turning that first serve into a point won.

And in a sport where a handful of points can decide an entire match, that difference can be everything.
